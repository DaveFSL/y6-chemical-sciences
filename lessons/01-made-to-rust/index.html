Made to rust

What evidence tells us a new substance has been made

AC9S6U04 AC9S6I02 90 minutes Term 4 · Week 1 Investigation launch

Teaching notes

Read before the lesson. Everything here is hidden from students when teacher mode is off.

Where this sits This is the launch of a seven-week unit. Two jobs: get students building the evidence criteria themselves rather than receiving them, and get the rust jars sealed before they leave the room. The rust investigation needs four full weeks to Week 5, so the setup cannot slide.

The one thing to protect

Do not pre-teach reversible and irreversible. They are introduced at the end of the station circuit, as names for categories the class has already built. Introduce them first and the whole lesson collapses into a labelling exercise where students look for the word rather than the evidence.

The two moments that matter

1 · When students name what they looked at

In the Criteria phase, the shift you want is from what happened to how I could tell. A student saying "it went brown" is describing. A student saying "it went brown and it won't go back, so something new is there" is reasoning. Write the class list in their words, not yours — it becomes the reference point for the whole unit and they need to own it.

2 · When someone argues with a classification

The candle station is deliberately ambiguous — the wax melts reversibly while the wick burns irreversibly. If nobody spots it, point at it. A genuine disagreement about a station is worth more than six correct sorts, because it forces the class to ask what evidence would settle it. That question is the whole unit in miniature.

Reading the diagnostic

The card sort is not marked. Photograph each pair's sort and look for three things:

  • Dissolving in the "gone for good" pile. Expect this from most of the class. It is corrected by measurement in Lesson 2, not by being told.
  • Sorting by appearance. Students who put "banana going brown" and "ice melting" in the same pile because both are visible changes are not yet reasoning from recoverability. Target these students in Lesson 2.
  • Melting and dissolving used interchangeably. Very common. Chart the difference explicitly in the Criteria phase if it appears.

Before the bell

  • Safety glasses on every student for the station circuit and the setup — no exceptions.
  • Set the steel wool in vinegar going first, before the hook, so it has rusted visibly by the time the last group reaches it.
  • Bread is entirely pre-prepared — three slices on a plate, untoasted, golden, blackened. No appliance at that station.
  • The candle is the only flame in the room and it is teacher-run. Water and a fire blanket within reach.
  • Photograph of the client's rusting asset loaded and ready.
If it rains The outdoor visit becomes a photograph. Take the picture on a dry day beforehand so you are never without it. The chat matters more than standing in front of the thing.

Overview

Students work out for themselves how to tell whether a change has made something new — then set up the investigation that runs for the next four weeks.

What we are doing today

There is a big rusty cross at the front of our school. Nobody has ever tried to clean it or stop it. Somebody chose for it to look like that.

Somewhere else at school there is something rusting that nobody wanted to rust, and [CLIENT NAME] would like it to stop. In seven weeks you are going to write and tell them what to do about it.

Today you start working out the difference between the two. First you sort twelve everyday changes into two piles: ones you could undo, and ones you could not. Then you go round six benches and watch six changes happen in front of you, and you write down what you actually see, hear and smell.

At the end you build the jars of nails that sit on the shelf for the next five weeks. Get them right today, because you do not get to start again.

Two placeholders to fill in before you teach this The client is a real person at school who has a rusting problem — the groundsman, the facilities officer, the sports coordinator, whoever it is. What we are learning

Learning intention

We are learning to work out what evidence tells us that a new substance has been made.

I will be successful when I can…

  • describe a change using more than one kind of observation
  • sort changes by whether I think the original could be recovered
  • explain which evidence I used to decide, not just what I decided
  • set up an investigation so that someone else could repeat it exactly

Stretch

  • I can explain why one piece of evidence is never enough on its own.
  • I can argue that a change belongs in both groups, and justify it.
Curriculum AC9S6U04 — compare reversible changes, including dissolving and changes of state, and irreversible changes, including cooking and rusting that produce new substances.
AC9S6I02 — plan and conduct repeatable investigations, including deciding the variables to be changed, measured and controlled; describing potential risks; planning for the safe use of equipment and materials.

Before we start, write one thing you want to get better at in this lesson. Nothing here is saved or sent anywhere — it is for you.

How the question sets work Every question set in this unit runs easy to hard, in order. The last one or two in each set supply no method — students decide the approach and justify it. The three-tier paths in each phase do the visible differentiation; the questions themselves escalate quietly.

Hook — Made to rust 12 min

Outside, at the entrance.

This cross has been here for years. It is covered in rust.

Nobody has ever tried to stop it. Somebody chose this.

Questions, in this order

  1. What do you notice about the surface?
  2. What do you think it looked like on the day it was installed?
  3. Could we get it back to that? How would we try?
  4. Why would anyone choose to let steel rust on purpose?
Leave question 4 unanswered Take the answer nowhere today. Write the question on the board and leave it there for five weeks. Students will return to it in Lesson 5 with a mass reading in front of them that explains it. Answering it now costs you the best payoff in the unit.

Then, inside

Show the photograph of the client’s rusting item — the one with rust nobody wants.

Same reaction. Completely different problem.

[CLIENT NAME] looks after this and would like it to stop. In seven weeks you are going to write to them and tell them what to do. Today you start finding out.

Why this framing works Two objects, one reaction, opposite verdicts. It blocks the easy conclusion that irreversible change is damage, and it gives the investigation a reader. Students behave differently when the recommendation goes to a person who could disagree with it.
A thought worth sitting with A cross made of something that changes permanently, and cannot be returned to what it was. Some things are meant to be changed for good.

Agree what counts as evidence 18 min

You have all made decisions. Now we work out how you made them.

The question

You put those cards in piles. What were you actually looking at when you decided?

Take answers. Write them on the board in the words students use. Group them as they repeat.

What classes usually come up with

Expect most of these, in roughly this order:

  • It changed colour
  • It made bubbles or a gas
  • It got hot or cold
  • It smells different now
  • There is something solid there that was not there before
  • You cannot undo it — this one usually arrives last and is the most important

If "you cannot undo it" does not come up, ask: what is the difference between the ice and the toast? Somebody will get there.

Use their words on the chart, not yours "It smells different" stays as "it smells different". This list goes on the wall for the whole term and gets refined each week. Students defend a list they wrote far more willingly than one they were given.

Then press on it

  1. Which of these would convince you on its own?
  2. Food colouring in water changes colour. Is that gone for good?
  3. So how many of these do you need before you are sure?
Where this is going

The food colouring example breaks the single-criterion rule, and it needs breaking today. Colour change alone proves nothing — you can evaporate coloured water and get the colour back.

The landing point is not a number. It is: one observation is a clue, several agreeing is evidence, and "can we get it back" is the one that decides it.

If melting and dissolving are being muddled Chart it now, quickly. Melting needs heat and involves one substance changing state. Dissolving needs a second substance and usually no heat at all. It will cause trouble in Lessons 2 and 3 if it goes unaddressed.

Station 5 is the one to protect The wax melts and resets; the wick burns away and does not come back. Same object, both categories at once. If a group notices unprompted, stop the circuit and let them tell the room. If nobody does, raise it in the summary — it is the strongest thing on the circuit.

Naming them

Now, and only now:

Reversible — the change can be undone and you get the original substance back.

Irreversible — a new substance has been made, and you cannot get the original back.

Which of your two piles is which?

Set up the investigation 17 min

These jars sit on the shelf for the next five weeks. Get them right now — you do not get to start again.

[CLIENT NAME] needs to know how to stop the rusting. Between the two Year 6 classes we are going to find out. Neither class can answer it alone.

ClassQuestion
TuesdayWhat makes rust happen? Six different conditions.
ThursdayWhat stops rust happening? Six different coatings, all in the same conditions.

Your group's job

  1. Collect three jars. Check each one for chips — if it is cracked, swap it. Your three must be the same size as each other; they do not have to match another group's.
  2. Weigh your three nails together. Write the mass on the label before they go in.
  3. Mark your nails before they go in. Put a marker line at the halfway point of each nail. That line, not the jar, is what "half covered" means.
  4. Set up your condition in all three jars, exactly the same way each time. Water to the marker line for half-submerged; at least 1 cm over the top of the nail for fully submerged.
  5. Mark the water level on the outside of each jar. Five weeks of standing water evaporates, and you will be topping it back up to that line every week.
  6. Label every jar: group, condition, nail mass, jar size, today's date.
  7. Write the one sentence that would let another group repeat your setup exactly.
Why three jars and not one If you only had one and something went wrong with it, you would never know. Three lets you spot the odd one out.
One condition per group Your group gets one of the conditions below, and all three of your jars run that same one. They are three copies of the same test, not three different tests. Six groups covers all six conditions, and we put them side by side in Week 5.

Conditions — Tuesday. What makes rust happen?

JarSet-up
ADry, sealed
BHalf-submerged in tap water, open
CFully submerged in tap water, open
DFully submerged in salt water — 2 teaspoons per 250 mL
EFully submerged in white vinegar
FBoiled and cooled water, sealed under 1 cm of cooking oil
Tuesday — the result that teaches B rusts fastest, not C. Rusting needs water and oxygen together and the waterline has both. F barely rusts at all. A and F are the pair that does the work: air without water, water without air, and neither rusts. Do not give this away — let the jars say it in Week 5.

Conditions — Thursday. What stops rust happening? Every jar is half-submerged in tap water. The only difference is what is on the nail.

JarSet-up
ABare nail — the control
BPainted
CPetroleum jelly
DWrapped in electrical tape
EClear nail polish
FGalvanised nail, untreated
Thursday — the result that teaches D often loses to A: a barrier with a gap in it traps water against the metal. F usually wins with no visible coating at all, because the zinc corrodes instead of the iron. "Cover it up" and "make the metal itself not rust" are two different answers to the client, and that distinction belongs in the Week 7 recommendation. Thursday's jar A is set up identically to Tuesday's jar B — the cross-check between the two classes.
If you cannot get three matching jars Control the nail, not the container. A marker line at the halfway point of the nail means "half covered" is identical in any jar, of any shape. For the fully submerged jars, measure the cover over the nail rather than filling to the top. Then write the jar size on the label so the other class knows what you used — and so you can say honestly, in Week 6, which of your variables you controlled and which you only recorded.
The sentence in step 5 is assessable This is your first read on AC9S6I02 — planning a repeatable investigation. A group writing "we put nails in water" has not done it. A group writing "three 65 mm nails, half covered in tap water, lid off, on the shelf by the window" has. Worth two minutes of feedback now, because the same skill is marked in CP2.
Safety Glasses on. Nails counted out and counted back in. Jars stay on the bench and are carried with two hands, one at a time. Any spill gets reported and cleaned straight away.

Jars go on the shelf. We look at them every week for the next four weeks — five minutes, no more.

Review 5 min

Three things to take away

  1. To tell whether something new has been made, you need more than one piece of evidence.
  2. The strongest question is: can we get it back?
  3. An irreversible change is not automatically a bad thing. Somebody wanted that cross to rust.

Exit ticket

Which station was hardest to classify, and what would settle it?

One sentence each. You are not being asked for the right answer — you are being asked what evidence you would want.

Sorting the exit tickets into piles

Ready to move on — names a station and proposes evidence that would actually settle it. "The candle, because I would need to know if the wax could be re-melted."

Watch in Lesson 2 — names a station but proposes no test, or proposes one that would not settle anything.

Target directly — says nothing was hard, or names a station for a reason unrelated to evidence. These students are not yet distinguishing observing from deciding.

Words we used today

reversibleirreversiblesubstance dissolveevidenceobservation rustrepeatable
EAL/D Four of these words mean something different outside science. Substance in everyday English often means importance. Change means coins. Solution means an answer to a problem. Observation means a remark someone made. Chart the science meaning next to the everyday one rather than assuming the word is new — for many students the problem is that it is familiar and wrong.
