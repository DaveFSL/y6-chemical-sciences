Reading the evidence

Five weeks of data, and somebody else's method to pull apart

AC9S6U04 AC9S6I04 AC9S6I05 90 minutes Term 4 · Week 5 ★ Assessment CP2

Teaching notes

Read before the lesson. Hidden from students when teacher mode is off.

Where this sits The biggest lesson in the unit, and the one everything since Week 1 has been building toward. The nails come out heavier, which is the single most convincing evidence a Year 6 student can personally generate that an irreversible change makes a new substance. Then each class pulls apart the other's method, which is the half of the evaluation criterion that cannot be faked. CP2 is written at the end.

The number that decides whether this works

Three 65 mm nails weigh about 13.8 g. A realistic mass gain over five weeks is:

Iron convertedMass gainedOn 0.01 g scales
0.5% — light rust0.03 g3 divisions
1%0.06 g6 divisions
2% — typical Jar B0.12 g12 divisions
5% — heavy rust0.30 g30 divisions

Readable, and comfortably so. On 1 g scales every one of those rounds to zero, which is why the 0.01 g scales matter today and nowhere else in the unit.

Drying is not fussiness — it is the experiment A single drop of water left on the nails weighs 0.05 g. That is five divisions — bigger than the entire signal from a lightly rusted jar. Half a millilitre is 50 divisions and would invent a result several times larger than the real one.

Dry properly: rinse briefly under the tap, blot hard with paper towel, then spread on a tray in the sun or in the frypan on low for a few minutes. Leave them until they look and feel completely dry, then leave them a bit longer. Weigh the Jar A dry control alongside — it should read within a division or two of its setup mass. If it has gained, your drying has failed and every other number is wrong.

The two moments that matter

1 · When the first group reads a bigger number

Stop the room. Ask what they expected. Almost everyone predicts lighter, because rust flakes off and looks like decay. Heavier is genuinely surprising. Then: where did the extra mass come from? Nothing was added to a sealed jar. It came out of the air, and it is now part of the nail. That is Lesson 2's logic running in reverse — mass went up, so something joined.

2 · When they get the other class's data

Students will critique another class in ways they will never critique their friends, and they cannot fall back on "I know what we meant." Insist on written critiques before any discussion. The writing is the assessment evidence; the discussion afterwards is where they find out whether they were right.

Running the weigh-in

  • Do the drying first, before anything else. Fifteen minutes of lesson time is the drying. Get nails out and onto trays in the first five minutes.
  • Tongs or gloves only. Rusted nails are sharp and carry a tetanus risk. Count them out and count them back in.
  • One weighing station. Two scales, six groups, three jars each — that is 18 weighings per class. Queue them; do not try to run it in parallel with other work.
  • Record to 0.01 g. Two decimal places, every time, including trailing zeros. 13.80 g is a different statement from 13.8 g.
  • Vinegar jars will misbehave. Jar E often loses mass, because acid dissolves iron away rather than just oxidising it. That is a real result and worth naming, not hiding.
If the drying fails or the scales have not arrived Run the whole analysis on the coverage ratings instead. They are five weeks of honest data and they answer the question perfectly well. Say plainly what went wrong and why the mass numbers cannot be trusted. A teacher modelling an honest account of a failed measurement is worth more here than clean data — and it is exactly the reasoning CP3 asks students to do.

What the data should show

Tuesday — what causes rustExpected
B — half-submergedMost rust. Water and oxygen together at the waterline.
D — salt waterClose second. Salt speeds up the reaction.
C — fully submergedLess than B. Limited oxygen.
E — vinegarErratic. May lose mass as iron dissolves.
A — dry sealedAlmost none. No water.
F — boiled water under oilAlmost none. No oxygen.

B beating C is the result that teaches. Students predicted more water means more rust. A and F together establish that rusting needs both water and oxygen — remove either and it stops.

Running CP2

  • Groups draft the plan together; each student submits their own.
  • The peer critique happens before submission and is written on the proforma. That box is assessable — it shows whether the student could take a criticism and act on it.
  • Approve every plan before Week 6. A plan you have not signed off is a group that cannot start.
  • Fifteen minutes is enough to draft, not to polish. Finishing at home or in a spare slot is fine.
The discriminator in CP2 An A-grade plan names controlled variables that would actually wreck the result — nail size, water volume, position on the shelf, start date. A C-grade plan lists whatever comes to mind. The difference is whether the student is thinking about their own method or reciting a category.

What we are learning

Learning intention

We are learning to turn our measurements into a graph that shows what the data actually says.

Your goal for today

The weigh-in 15 min

Five weeks. Let us find out.

Safety first Rusted nails are sharp and rust carries a tetanus risk. Tongs or gloves only — never bare hands. Count your nails out and count them back in. Tell the teacher immediately if anyone gets scratched.

Getting them dry

  1. Lift your nails out with tongs. Keep each jar's three together and labelled.
  2. Rinse briefly under the tap.
  3. Blot hard with paper towel. Then blot again.
  4. Spread them on your tray and leave them to dry properly.

Why we are being this fussy about drying.

One drop of water weighs 0.05 g. The mass your nails have actually gained might only be 0.06 g. So a single drop left behind could double your result and you would never know. Water is not rust, and the scale cannot tell the difference.

Weighing

  1. Wait until the nails look and feel completely dry.
  2. Weigh all three together, exactly as you did in Week 1.
  3. Record to two decimal places, always — 13.80 g, not 13.8 g.
  4. Subtract the setup mass. That difference is your result.
The check that tells you whether to trust any of it The dry sealed jar should weigh what it weighed in Week 1, give or take a division. If it has gained, the drying has failed and every other number in the class is too high.

What happened?

  1. Did your nails get heavier, lighter, or stay the same?
  2. Is that what you expected five weeks ago?
  3. Nothing was added to your jar. So where did the extra mass come from?
Show the answers

1. Heavier — most conditions gain between 0.03 g and 0.30 g. The vinegar jar may lose mass, because acid dissolves iron away rather than just rusting it.

2. Almost nobody expects heavier. Rust flakes and looks like decay, so lighter feels obvious.

3. Out of the air. Rust is iron joined to oxygen, and the oxygen came from inside the jar. The nail is now iron plus something it collected — which is exactly why it weighs more. In Lesson 2 the mass dropped when a gas escaped. Here it rose because a gas joined.

This is the payoff of the whole unit Week 2 established that mass is conserved and a loss means something left. Here mass increases, which means something joined. The oxygen came from the air and is now part of the nail. It is the single most convincing piece of evidence a Year 6 student can personally generate that an irreversible change makes a new substance — and they generated it themselves over five weeks.

Graph it 20 min

Your three jars first

Plot your own condition. Three bars, one per jar.

  1. Are your three bars the same height?
  2. Should they be? You set all three up identically.
  3. How different would two jars have to be before you would say it was a real difference rather than just wobble?
Why we ran three If you had only run one jar, you would have no idea whether your number was typical or a fluke. Three tells you how much your own results wobble — and that tells you how big a difference has to be before it means anything.

Then the whole class

Six conditions on one graph. Use the average of each group's three jars.

  1. Which condition rusted the most?
  2. Which two conditions barely rusted at all? What do they have in common?
  3. Compare the half-submerged jar with the fully submerged one. Which won?
  4. Is that what you predicted in Week 1?
Show the answers — Tuesday, what causes rust

1. Jar B, half-submerged, usually by a clear margin. Salt water (D) is often close behind.

2. Jar A (dry, sealed) and Jar F (boiled water under oil). A had air but no water. F had water but no oxygen. Neither rusted, and between them they prove you need both.

3. Half-submerged beats fully submerged — which surprises nearly everyone.

4. Most people predicted more water means more rust. The waterline is where water and oxygen meet, and rusting needs both at once. Under the surface there is far less oxygen available.

Show the answers — Thursday, what stops rust

1. The bare control, by a long way. That is the point — everything else was trying to beat it.

2. The galvanised nail and whichever coating stayed intact. A tape or polish coating with a gap in it often does worse than expected, because water gets underneath and cannot dry out.

3–4. The useful surprise is that a damaged barrier can be worse than no barrier at all. That matters for the client's recommendation: a coating only works if it is complete and stays complete.

Draw out the A and F pair deliberately Two conditions that both failed to rust, for opposite reasons. Together they are a controlled experiment in miniature: remove water, nothing happens; remove oxygen, nothing happens. That is far stronger evidence than the winner, and students tend to skip straight past it to look at which bar is tallest.
Need a hand
  • Use the pre-scaled graph frame.
  • Plot your three jars, then the class averages.
Keep going
  • Build and scale both graphs yourself.
  • Write the pattern in words underneath.
Push me
  • Express the gain as a percentage of the starting mass. Does that change which condition won?
  • Is the gap between conditions bigger than the gap between your own three jars? What does your answer mean?

The cross explained 20 min

First — what actually happened to your nails

Iron + oxygen → iron oxide. That is rust.

The oxygen came out of the air in your jar. It is now locked into the surface of the nail, which is why the nail weighs more than the steel you started with. A new substance was made, and it was made from the nail.

  1. Why can you not scrub rust off and get your nail back to new?
  2. Rust flakes off and exposes fresh metal underneath. What happens next?
Show the answers

1. Because the rust is not sitting on the nail — it is made of the nail. Scrub it off and you take some of the nail with it. The iron that turned into rust is not coming back.

2. It rusts too. Then that flakes off, and the metal underneath rusts. On ordinary steel this never stops. Given enough time, there is no steel left.

Now — the question from Week 1

The cross at our entrance has been rusting for years. Nobody has ever tried to stop it.

Somebody chose that. Why?

It is made from weathering steel, which has other metals mixed into it. It rusts like any steel — but then something different happens.

Ordinary steel1

Rust forms. It is flaky and loose.

It falls off, exposing clean metal.

That rusts too. It never stops.

Your client's asset is doing this right now.
Weathering steel2

Rust forms. It is tight and it sticks.

It seals the surface instead of falling off.

Water and air cannot get past it, so it stops.

The cross at our entrance.
  1. Both are irreversible changes. Why is one a problem and the other a solution?
  2. So does knowing a change is irreversible tell you whether it is bad?
  3. What would you need to know to decide?
Show the answers

1. Identical chemistry — iron and oxygen making iron oxide. The difference is what the rust does once it has formed. Loose rust keeps the reaction going. Tight rust shuts it down.

2. No. Irreversible means it cannot be undone. It says nothing about whether you wanted it.

3. What the change is for. Cooking, setting concrete and curing glue are all irreversible and all deliberate. The cross was designed to do this. The gate was not.

The idea this whole unit is built on An irreversible change can be exactly what you want — if you understand it well enough to control it. The people who built that cross knew precisely what would happen to it, and chose it anyway.
Optional — worth the two minutes if the room is with you A cross made of something that changes permanently and cannot be returned to what it was, placed at the entrance. You are already having this conversation when you take them out there. Offer it as a question rather than a point: why might someone choose that material for that object?

★ Investigation Plan 15 min

Assessment. Drafted in your group, critiqued by another, submitted individually.

Next week you run your own investigation, and the results go to [CLIENT NAME].

The shared question is how to protect metal from rusting. Your group picks what to change. Your plan has to be good enough that another group could run it without asking you anything.

What the plan needs

  • Your question — naming what you will change and what you will measure
  • Your prediction, and the evidence from the last five weeks that led you to it
  • Your variables: changed, measured, and at least three controlled
  • Your method, numbered
  • How many times you will run it, and why that many
  • Your risks and what you will do about them
  • Your equipment
  • What another group asked, and what you changed because of it
The part people lose marks on Controlled variables. Do not list everything you can think of. List the things that would actually wreck your result if they were different — nail size, how much water, where the jars sit, what day you start. Ask yourself: if this changed, would my answer change?
You have done this before In Week 3 you drafted a method, handed it to another group and got one hard question back. Same protocol, higher stakes — and next week you hand your graphs to the other class for exactly this treatment. What would you attack?

Swap your plan

Draft it, hand it to another group, and get one hard question back. Write down what they asked and what you changed. That box is part of the assessment — it shows whether you can take a criticism and use it.
