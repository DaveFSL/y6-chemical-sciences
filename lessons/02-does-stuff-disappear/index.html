Does stuff disappear?

Using mass to find out whether anything new has been made

AC9S6U04 AC9S6I03 AC9S6I01 90 minutes Term 4 · Week 2 Measurement

Teaching notes

Read before the lesson. Hidden from students when teacher mode is off.

Where this sits This is the conceptual pivot of the whole unit. Students arrive believing two things that are both wrong — that dissolving destroys a substance, and that fizzing makes something out of nothing. Both are corrected today, by the same instrument, in the same lesson. Everything after this rests on it.

The one thing to protect

Students must commit a number in writing before anything is weighed. Not a guess said aloud, not a show of hands — a number on paper with their name on it. The learning comes from the gap between what they wrote and what the scale says, and if they never committed, there is no gap.

The corollary: do not tell them mass is conserved. That sentence is the answer to today's question and it is theirs to reach.

The two moments that matter

1 · When the salt result comes in

Most of the class will have predicted lighter. The scale says the same. Do not resolve it quickly — sit in it. Ask: "so where is the salt?" Someone will say it is still in the water. Ask how they could prove that. Park the answer; Lesson 3 is them proving it.

2 · When the two containers disagree

Same reaction, same ingredients, two containers. The sealed bag holds its mass; the open beaker loses about three grams. Students have to account for the difference themselves. The answer — that something left the open one — is only available to a class that has already accepted the gas is a real substance with real mass.

Method that decides whether this works

  • Weigh the whole system, including the containers. Beaker of water AND the cup of salt both go on the scale together. Tip the salt in, then put the empty cup back on. Same total. This is the same logic as the sealed bag, so use it in both — consistency between the two activities is what lets students transfer the idea.
  • Cover the beaker. Warm water in an open container loses measurable mass to evaporation inside twenty minutes. A drifting reading in a lesson about conserved mass is the worst possible confound. Cling film or a lid.
  • Keep everything under 400 g. The scales max out at 500 g including the container.
  • Let the fizzing finish. The visible reaction stops in about a minute but degassing continues. Swirl gently, wait five minutes, then reweigh.
Bag size and quantities are not negotiable The sealed bag gets 50 mL of vinegar and 2 g of bicarbonate, in a 2-litre freezer bag — that produces about 0.6 L of gas, which inflates the bag clearly without stressing the seal. Do not scale it up. The open-beaker quantities (100 mL and 6 g) would make about 1.6 litres, which would burst a sandwich bag. Those quantities are only ever used in an open container.

Reading the results

What they doExpectWhat it tells you
Salt into waterNo changeNothing was destroyed. Dissolving is a reversible change.
Sealed bag fizzingNo change, bag inflatesThe gas is inside and it weighs something. Nothing was created either.
Open beaker fizzingAbout 3 g lighterSomething left. It went into the room.

Groups will not all land on exactly 3 g — expect 2.2 to 2.9. Some carbon dioxide stays dissolved in the liquid, and a little is lost as spray. That spread is useful: it is the first time this unit that students see real measurements disagreeing, and it previews the error discussion in Week 5.

On precision

Worth explicit teaching The elaboration asks for data recorded with appropriate precision — appropriate to the instrument, not the maximum number of decimal places available. A student writing 120.00 g off a scale that reads whole grams has made an error, not shown rigour. Say it out loud today; it is assessed in CP3 under Finding.

Misconceptions to expect

MisconceptionHow it shows todayResponse
Dissolving destroys the substancePredicting the salt solution will weigh less than the totalThe scale. Then ask how they would prove the salt is still there — and let Lesson 3 answer it.
Gas is nothing"It turned into nothing" when the bag is discussedThe inflated bag is clearly full of something, and the sealed mass has not dropped. It is in there and it weighs something.
Mass is created by the reactionPredicting the fizzing container will get heavierNeither container gains. One holds, one loses.
Bubbles always mean a new substanceCarried over from Lesson 1's boiling water cardBoiling makes bubbles of the same stuff. This makes bubbles of something that was not there before — and you can tell because the mass drops.
More decimal places is more accurateWriting 118.00 g from a whole-gram scalePrecision belongs to the instrument. Record what it actually gives you.

What we are learning

Learning intention

We are learning to use mass to work out whether anything new has been made.

I will be successful when I can…

  • use the scales accurately and record to the precision they actually allow
  • make a prediction, then say honestly whether my evidence supported it
  • explain why the mass stays the same when something dissolves
  • explain why the mass drops when a gas escapes from an open container

Stretch

  • I can predict the mass loss in grams before it happens, and account for the difference.
  • I can explain why writing more decimal places than my instrument gives is an error, not extra care.
Curriculum AC9S6U04 — compare reversible changes, including dissolving and changes of state, and irreversible changes, including cooking and rusting that produce new substances.
AC9S6I03 — use equipment to observe, measure and record data with reasonable precision, using digital tools as appropriate.
AC9S6I01 — pose investigable questions to identify patterns and test relationships and make reasoned predictions.

Rust check 5 min

Week 1 reading. Five minutes, then we move on.

Photograph your three jars. Rate each one 0 to 4 against the scale. Write it in the log. Rate all three separately — do not average them in your head.

  1. Collect your log sheet and find your jars. Do not move them.
  2. Photograph all three from the same angle as last week.
  3. Rate each jar against the laminated scale.
  4. Top the open jars back up to the marked water line, and tick the log to say you did.
  5. Write anything you notice in the last column.
If your three jars disagree That is data, not a mistake. Write all three numbers down and think about why they might differ.
Keep this to five minutes No discussion of results yet. The temptation is to let a good conversation run, but the analysis belongs in Week 5 when there is a pattern to analyse. One week's data is noise. Photograph, rate, record, sit down.
What you should be seeing After one week, jars B and E are usually showing traces or patches. A, C, D and F often read zero. Do not comment on it — students noticing early that B is ahead of C is exactly the surprise you want intact for Week 5.

Predict 10 min

On the scale: a beaker holding 100 g of warm water, and next to it a cup holding 20 g of salt. Together they read a number.

Now the salt gets tipped in and stirred until it vanishes. The empty cup goes back on the scale.

What will the scale read now?

  1. Heavier, lighter, or the same?
  2. Write the actual number you expect.
  3. Write why. One sentence.
Commit it in writing Write your number down with your name on it before anything is weighed. You are not being marked on whether you are right. You are being marked on whether you can say what happened to your idea afterwards.
Do not take a show of hands A show of hands lets students change their mind silently once they see the room. Written, individual, before any equipment moves. Then walk the room and note roughly how the class has split — you will use that number in the summary.
What to expect In most Year 6 classes, over half predict lighter. The reasoning is usually some version of "the salt disappeared, so it is gone". A few predict heavier. Almost nobody predicts the same without having been taught it. If your class predicts the same overwhelmingly, ask them to justify it — some will be guessing that the teacher wants that answer.
Need a hand
  • Choose from heavier, lighter or the same.
  • Then finish: "I think this because…"
Keep going
  • Write a number in grams.
  • Write one sentence of reasoning.
Push me
  • Predict the fizzing results too, both containers, before you see either.

Dissolving test 20 min

Safety glasses on. Everything stays on the bench.

Method

  1. Put the beaker of warm water on the scale. Put the cup of salt on beside it. Record the total.
  2. Take both off. Tip the salt into the water and stir until you cannot see it.
  3. Cover the beaker.
  4. Put the beaker back on the scale — and the empty cup with it. Record the total.
Why the empty cup goes back on You are weighing everything you started with. If you leave the cup off, you are weighing less stuff than before and the test proves nothing. Nothing leaves the scale.
Why we cover it Warm water evaporates. Over twenty minutes that is enough mass to show up and it would ruin the result.

Then answer

  1. What did the scale read before? What did it read after?
  2. Was your prediction right? Write what actually happened.
  3. You cannot see the salt any more. So where is it?
  4. How could you prove it is still there?
Show the answers

1. The same number, both times. Small differences of a gram or two come from splashes and from the scale rounding.

2. Most predictions will have been lighter. The honest answer is "I thought it would be lighter and it was not."

3. Still in the water, in pieces far too small to see. Nothing was destroyed — it was spread out.

4. Get it back out. Take the water away and see whether the salt is still there. That is next week.

Do not answer question 4 Take suggestions, write the best one on the board, and stop there. Boiling it off, leaving it in the sun, and filtering are all worth hearing. Next week they do it and weigh what comes back. Answering it now spends Lesson 3 before you get there.
Watch for Groups who record a number that differs by more than about five grams have almost certainly left something off the scale. Check the cup is back on. This is a good live teaching point about weighing the whole system.

Fizzing test 25 min

Same reaction, two containers. Watch what the scale does differently.

Container A — sealedA

50 mL vinegar in one corner of the freezer bag. 2 g bicarbonate in the other corner. Seal it, keeping them apart.

Weigh the whole sealed bag. Record.

Tip it so they mix — without opening it. Let it finish.

Weigh the whole sealed bag again. Record.

2 L freezer bag, 50 mL vinegar, 2 g bicarbonate
Container B — openB

100 mL vinegar in the tall beaker. 6 g bicarbonate in its cup beside it, on the scale.

Weigh both together. Record.

Tip the bicarbonate in. Put the empty cup back on the scale. Leave it open.

Swirl gently. Wait five minutes. Weigh again. Record.

Tall beaker or flask, 100 mL vinegar, 6 g bicarbonate, cup
Do not open the bag Not to look, not to let the pressure out, not at the end. It stays sealed until after both readings are recorded. Use the 2-litre freezer bag, not a sandwich bag.

What happened?

ContainerMass beforeMass afterDifference
A — sealed
B — open
  1. Which container changed mass?
  2. Both had exactly the same reaction happening. Why did only one change?
  3. The bag puffed up. What is inside it, and does it weigh anything?
  4. Where has the missing mass from container B actually gone?
Show the answers

1. Only B, the open one. It loses roughly 2 to 3 grams.

2. In A nothing could get out. In B the gas escaped into the room. The reaction was identical — the container was not.

3. A gas, made by the reaction. It was not there before. And it weighs something, which is why A did not get lighter — the gas is still on the scale.

4. Into the air in the classroom. It has not been destroyed and it has not become nothing. It has moved somewhere we cannot weigh.

This is the pairing that teaches Neither container proves anything alone. Sealed on its own looks like nothing happened. Open on its own looks like matter was destroyed. Side by side they force the only explanation that fits both: a new substance was made, it is a gas, and gas has mass.
Expect a spread, and use it Groups will get between about 2.2 g and 2.9 g for container B rather than an identical figure. Some carbon dioxide stays dissolved in the liquid and a little is lost as spray. Pool the numbers on the board. This is the first time this unit that students see real measurements disagreeing — name it, and say that working out why measurements disagree is what Week 5 is about.
Need a hand
  • A partner reads the scale aloud while you write.
  • Use the table with the steps already filled in.

  • Run both containers, record all four masses.
  • Answer all four questions.

Summarise 20 min

Pool the numbers

Every group's container B loss goes on the board.

  1. Are they all the same? Should they be?
  2. What could make one group's number different from another's?
Do not tidy this up Resist averaging the numbers into one clean figure. The spread is real and it is the honest picture. Ask what could cause it — spray leaving the beaker, gas still dissolved, not waiting long enough, scale rounding. Every one of those is a source of error, which is the exact language of the Week 5 assessment.

The two big ideas

Nothing disappears

When salt dissolves, the mass does not change. The salt is still there — spread through the water in pieces too small to see. Nothing was destroyed, so nothing new was made. That is a reversible change.

If it got lighter, something left

When the mass drops in an open container, a substance has escaped — usually as a gas. That gas was not there before, which means something new was made. That is an irreversible change.

Back to the chart

Last week you listed the evidence that something new has been made. Add the one nobody suggested:

The mass changed.

  1. Where does this belong on our list — near the top or near the bottom?
  2. Is it stronger evidence than colour change? Why?
Where this is going

Mass is stronger than colour, smell, or bubbles, for one reason: it is a number. Two people looking at the same brown liquid can disagree about how brown it is. Two people reading the same scale cannot.

It is not perfect — the spread on the board shows that. But it can be checked, repeated, and compared between groups in a way that "it looked different" never can.

Amend the chart in front of them Physically write it on. Students need to see the list being changed by evidence they generated, because in Lesson 4 they amend it again. A chart that gets edited is a working tool; a chart that goes up in Week 1 and never changes is wallpaper.

EAL/D Solution is the one to chart. In everyday English it means the answer to a problem; in science it means a substance dissolved evenly through a liquid. Review 10 min

Three things to take away

  1. Dissolving does not destroy anything. The mass proves it.
  2. If a sealed container keeps its mass, nothing has left — even if the reaction looks dramatic.
  3. If an open container loses mass, something has escaped, and it was made by the reaction.

Exit ticket

A sealed jar of fizzing tablets weighs exactly the same before and after the fizzing stops.

A student says: "That proves nothing happened."

Are they right? How do you know?

Sorting the exit tickets into piles

Ready to move on — says the student is wrong, and explains that the gas is still inside the jar so it still counts on the scale. Bonus if they mention that opening it would drop the mass.

Watch in Lesson 3 — says the student is wrong but the reason is thin: "because something did happen" or "because we saw bubbles". Right conclusion, no mechanism.

Target directly — agrees with the student. These students have not yet accepted that a gas is a substance with mass, and Lesson 3's recovery work will not land until they do. Worth two minutes each next week.
