The misconception this lesson exists to break

Students believe irreversible change requires extreme heat. Four weeks of burning, rusting and fizzing has quietly reinforced it. Two numbers undo it:

ChangeTemperatureVerdict
Chocolate melts34–38 °CReversible. Body temperature is 37 °C — it melts in your mouth by design.
Egg white setsabout 63 °CIrreversible. Well below boiling.
Water boils100 °CReversible. Hotter than both, and reverses completely.

An egg white cannot be un-cooked at a temperature where water is still comfortably liquid. Temperature is not the thing. What the substance is is the thing — and how much energy goes in decides which of its thresholds you cross.

Worth saying out loud about the chocolate Cocoa butter is one of very few fats that melts in the narrow band just below mouth temperature, and confectioners choose it for exactly that. Chocolate melting in your hand is not an accident or a flaw — it is the property the whole product is built around. A material selected for how it behaves when heated, which is the same idea as the resin adhesive in Lesson 3.

The two moments that matter

1 · When the sugar goes brown

There is a specific moment when it stops being sugar. Students mark it on their sheet as it happens, in real time, and commit to evidence. The ones to look for are those citing more than one thing — it went brown and it smells different and it will not go back when it cools. A student citing colour alone has a rule, not an understanding.

2 · When the egg white number goes on the board

Sixty-three degrees. Below boiling. Ask whether anyone can un-cook it. Then ask how a change that needs so little heat can be more permanent than boiling water. Sit in the discomfort — that is the concept arriving.

Running the sugar

  • Two tablespoons, spread thin. More than that and the middle burns before the edges melt. Small quantity also limits the smoke.
  • Medium-high. A frypan tops out around 200–230 °C, which reaches caramelisation at about 170 °C but does not race past it. If you use a hotplate instead, run it lower than you think — 300 °C-plus overshoots to black very quickly.
  • Do not stir at first. Let it go clear from the edges. Stirring early makes it seize into lumps.
  • Never put a thermometer probe into molten sugar. It is over 180 °C, it sticks, and it will drag hot sugar with it when removed. Read the pan setting and observe the sugar by eye. The probe is for the chocolate and the egg white, where it is safe and the numbers actually matter.
  • Stop at the first serious smoke. You have the result you need once it is dark brown. Black and smoking adds nothing except a smoke alarm.
The honest nuance on "melting" sugar Sugar does not cleanly melt the way ice does — decomposition begins around the same temperature as the apparent melting. For Year 6 the observable is what matters and it is sound: goes clear and runny, then sets hard again when it cools — still sugar, versus goes brown and smells different — not sugar any more. If a sharp student asks whether the clear stage is really reversible, that is an excellent question and the honest answer is "mostly, and it is more complicated than it looks." Do not pretend it is simple.
Safety — the highest-risk demonstration in the unit Molten sugar is above 180 °C and sticks to skin, which makes its burns far worse than boiling water. Strictly teacher-operated. One-metre exclusion line, students seated behind it for the whole demonstration. Safety glasses and heat-resistant gloves for you. Long hair tied back. Windows and door open before heating starts, and check where the smoke detector is — notify the office if it is nearby. Cold running water immediately available. Pan stays where it is to cool; do not carry hot residue across the room.
Allergens Egg today, and chocolate contains milk. Check both classes' allergy registers before you buy anything, and again on the morning. If anyone in either class is at risk, the sugar sequence carries the whole lesson on its own — the chocolate and egg pairing can be replaced with butter and a description of the egg result without losing the concept.

The discriminator in Part B A B-grade response describes change one, then change two. An A-grade response picks a criterion — can you get it back, did the mass alter, was a new substance made — and runs both changes through it. That structural difference is the clearest signal of purposeful comparison at this year level, and it is what separates thorough from informed in the elaborations.

Overview

Heat something gently and it comes back. Heat it further and it does not. Today you find the moment in between.

What we are doing today

Everything gets heated today, and your job is to spot the moment a change stops being one you could undo.

Sugar goes in the pan first. It melts, then turns golden, then brown, then black and smoky. Somewhere along the way it stops being sugar. You decide where, and you say what convinced you.

Then chocolate and egg white, with a thermometer in each. Chocolate melts at about the temperature of your hand, and sets hard again when it cools. Egg white sets at around 63 degrees and never goes back — and that is cooler than boiling water, which you can undo. So the temperature on its own does not tell you which kind of change you are looking at.

The last half hour is your first assessment task.

What we are learning

Learning intention

We are learning to compare a reversible and an irreversible change caused by adding heat.

I will be successful when I can…

  • describe what happens to a substance as more and more heat is added
  • identify the point at which a new substance has been made
  • classify a change and justify it with evidence, not with a rule
  • compare a reversible and an irreversible change

Stretch

  • I can explain why temperature on its own does not tell you whether a change is reversible.
  • I can organise a comparison around a criterion instead of describing each change in turn.
Curriculum AC9S6U04 — compare reversible changes, including dissolving and changes of state, and irreversible changes, including cooking and rusting that produce new substances. The sugar escalation 30 min

One substance. More and more heat. Watch for the moment it stops being sugar.

Watch only Everybody stays behind the line, seated, for the whole demonstration. Melted sugar is hotter than boiling water and it sticks to skin. Nobody comes forward for any reason.

Your job

Record what you see, hear and smell at each stage.

Then mark the exact stage where you believe sugar stopped existing — and write the evidence that convinced you.

Running the sugar demonstration — step by step

On the bench: electric frypan, 2 tablespoons of white sugar in a small dish, a metal spoon, two saucers labelled stage 3 and stage 5, a heat-resistant mat, a wooden spoon for stirring, and the fire blanket within reach. Students seated behind the one-metre line.

  1. Before you switch on — tip the sugar into the cold frypan and spread it thin, no deeper than a few millimetres. Deep sugar burns at the bottom before the top has melted and you lose the middle stages. Point out the grainy white crystals: that is stage 1, and they record it now.
  2. Switch on, medium. Say out loud that nothing goes in or out of the pan from here. The whole thing takes about eight minutes, so keep talking and keep them recording.
  3. Call each stage as it arrives — "edges going clear, that's two", and so on. Give them fifteen seconds to write before you move on. Do not rush from 3 to 5; that stretch is the lesson.
  4. At stage 3, all liquid and still colourless — lift a spoonful onto the saucer marked stage 3 and set it aside to cool. Say what you are doing and why.
  5. At stage 5, definite amber — lift a second spoonful onto the saucer marked stage 5. The caramel smell should be obvious across the room by now. Ask them to write down what they can smell, because that is the evidence that matters.
  6. Take it to stage 6 — dark going black, sharp bitter smell, first wisps of smoke — then switch off and slide the pan off the element. Do not chase a blacker result. Once smoke appears you have what you need.
  7. While it cools, the two saucers go round the room on a tray, not in hands. Five minutes is enough for them to set hard. Nobody tastes anything.
  8. Leave the pan to cool completely before it goes near water. Hot sugar and water spits.

Thermometer stays out of the sugar. Molten sugar is over 180 °C, it sticks to the probe and drags hot sugar with it when you lift it. The temperatures in this lesson come from the chocolate and the egg, not the sugar.

The stages

StageWhat is happeningWhat to look for
1Cold sugarWhite, grainy, separate crystals. Smells of almost nothing.
2Edges go clearThe outside turns glassy and starts to run. Still colourless.
3All liquidClear, runny, like syrup. Bubbles at the edges.
4First colourA straw tint creeping in. The smell begins to change.
5AmberDefinite brown. Strong caramel smell across the room.
6DarkDeep brown going black. Sharp, bitter smell. First wisps of smoke.

The test

Answer on your sheet Part 1 and Part 2 of your Lesson 4 sheet in OneNote — the stage table, the stage where sugar stopped existing, and the two cooled samples.

A spoonful is taken out at stage 3 and left to cool. Another is taken out at stage 5.

Once they are cool: which one is still sugar?

  1. What did the stage 3 sample look like once it cooled?
  2. What did the stage 5 sample look like?
  3. Which stage did you mark as the point of no return, and why?
  4. The sugar got liquid at stage 3 and it got liquid at stage 5. Why is only one of those reversible?
  5. If you kept heating past stage 6, would you ever get sugar back?
Show the answers

1. Hard, clear or slightly cloudy, glassy. It looks different from the grainy powder it started as, but it is still sugar — it tastes of sugar and nothing new has been made.

2. Hard, brown, and it smells of caramel rather than of nothing. It is not sugar any more.

3. Somewhere between stage 4 and stage 5. The evidence to look for is more than one thing at once: the colour changed and the smell changed and it does not go back.

4. At stage 3 the sugar changed state — same substance, different form, and cooling reverses it. By stage 5 the heat has broken the sugar apart and built new substances, which is why it smells different. A change of state can be undone. A new substance cannot be un-made.

5. No. It keeps decomposing until what is left is mostly carbon — the black stuff. Every step takes it further away.

This is the elaboration, exactly "Comparing how the amount of heat energy added affects whether a change in state or an irreversible change occurs." One substance, one heat source, two completely different outcomes decided purely by how much energy went in. There is no cleaner demonstration of it available in a primary classroom.
If a student asks whether stage 3 is really reversible Excellent question, and the honest answer is "mostly, and it is more complicated than it looks." Sugar does not cleanly melt the way ice does — some decomposition starts around the same temperature. Do not pretend it is simple. Saying "you have found the edge of what this model handles" respects the question and is true.

Running the two temperature demonstrations — step by step

On the bench: the frypan on low, two small metal dishes, one digital thermometer per dish if you have two, a few pieces of broken chocolate, one egg white in a cup, and a spoon. Same exclusion line, students seated.

Chocolate first — about 5 minutes

  1. Three or four small pieces in the metal dish, thermometer probe resting in among them, dish into the frypan on low. Low matters: chocolate scorches and then you are demonstrating the wrong change.
  2. Read the temperature aloud every thirty seconds and have them write the numbers as you go.
  3. The moment the pieces lose their edges and slump, call the temperature. Expect somewhere between 34 and 38 °C. That is the number that goes on their sheet.
  4. Dish out of the pan, onto the mat. Say you will come back to it — it needs a few minutes to set again, and it will.

Egg white second — about 6 minutes

  1. A tablespoon of egg white into the second dish, probe in the middle of it, into the frypan on low.
  2. Read the temperature aloud as it climbs. It will sit around 50-something for a while and look like nothing is happening. Let that silence run — the contrast with what comes next is the point.
  3. The instant it turns opaque white, call the temperature. Expect about 63 °C. Write it on the board beside 100 °C for boiling water, and leave both numbers there through CP1.
  4. Out of the pan, onto the mat, next to the chocolate.

Then show both — the chocolate has hardened again, and the egg has not gone anywhere. Carry them round on the tray. That side-by-side is what makes the point, so do not skip it for time.

If a thermometer reads oddly, say so rather than adjusting the number. An honest "that reading does not look right, let's take it again" is worth more than a tidy figure, and Week 5 is built on trusting your instrument.

  1. Which of these needed the least heat?
  2. Which one is permanent?

  4. Does knowing the temperature tell you whether a change is reversible?
Show the answers

1. The chocolate, somewhere around 34–38 °C. Body temperature is 37 °C, so it melts at roughly the temperature of your own mouth — which is exactly why it melts in your hand and on your tongue.

2. The egg white, at around 63 °C. Still well below boiling.

3. Because boiling only changes water's state — it is still water, whether liquid or steam, and cooling brings it straight back. Cooking the egg builds something new out of what was there. Cooling cannot un-build it.

4. No. Not on its own. Temperature tells you how much energy went in. What happens depends on the substance and on which of its thresholds that energy crosses.

An egg cannot be un-cooked at a temperature where water is still perfectly happy being liquid.

Why chocolate melts in your hand Your body sits at about 37 °C, and chocolate melts somewhere around 34 to 38 °C. That is not a coincidence. The fat in chocolate — cocoa butter — is one of the very few that melts in that narrow band, and chocolate makers use it for precisely that reason. Melting in your mouth is the whole point of the product.
This is the whole point of the phase Four weeks of burning, rusting and fizzing has quietly taught the class that irreversible means extreme. Sixty-three degrees against a hundred breaks it in one line. Write both numbers on the board and leave them there through CP1.
Thermometer use The probe goes into the chocolate and the egg white, where it is safe and the readings matter. It never goes into molten sugar — that is over 180 °C, it sticks, and it drags hot sugar out with it.

Where this is going

Heat on its own is not evidence — ice melts when it is heated and nothing new is made. What today showed is subtler and more useful:

Adding heat can cause either kind of change. What decides it is the substance, and how much energy goes in.

Physically edit the chart in front of them Cross the old line out, write the new one. Students need to see a list being changed by evidence they generated — that is what makes it a working tool rather than wallpaper. It is also the last edit before the assessment, so leave it visible.

Where we have got to

  1. To tell whether something new was made, you need more than one piece of evidence.
  2. Mass is the strongest evidence, because it is a number rather than an opinion.
  3. The clearest question is still: can we get it back?
  4. Heat can cause either kind of change. How much you add decides which.

Assessment. Individual, silent, books closed.

You are not being tested on remembering. The class charts and your own results from Weeks 2 and 3 stay on display. You are being assessed on whether you can classify a change and say what evidence decided it — and whether you can compare two changes properly.
