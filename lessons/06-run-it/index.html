Teaching notes

Read before the lesson. Hidden from students when teacher mode is off.

Where this sits Students run the plans they wrote last week. Your job today is not to teach — it is to watch and record. The Finding criterion in CP3 is judged almost entirely from what you see at the bench, because whether a student reads a scale properly and writes down the unexpected does not survive into a write-up. The observation grid is the only evidence for it.

Why steel wool and not nails

A nail exposes about 1.3 cm² of steel per gram. Steel wool grade 0000 exposes around 200 cm² per gram — roughly 160 times more surface. What takes a nail five weeks takes steel wool one lesson.

A short vinegar soak strips the protective oxide layer so the reaction starts immediately rather than after a delay. Rust is then visible within about twenty minutes, and the pads keep going over the following week for a second reading at the start of Lesson 7.

The bonus measurable Rusting is exothermic. A vinegar-soaked pad, squeezed out and sealed in a jar with a thermometer, warms by 5 to 10 °C over fifteen minutes. Run this centrally as one demonstration with a thermometer and log the temperature on the board — you only have two thermometers, so six groups cannot each do it.

It is worth the bench space because it turns "it got hot" from the Lesson 1 criteria list into a number, and because some students will not believe rusting is a reaction until they see the temperature move.

The one instruction that matters most

Resist rescuing

A group whose method fails has generated genuine evidence for the evaluation criterion. Note what happened, let it run, and make sure they write it down as it happens. The written account of what went wrong is worth more in CP3 than a tidy result — and a group you quietly fixed has learnt nothing and given you nothing to assess.

The exception is safety. Step in immediately for anything unsafe, without discussion.

Running the observation grid

  • One page per group, on a clipboard. Print before the lesson with names filled in.
  • You are looking for four things: the safety statement before equipment is issued, whether the method is actually followed, precision of measurement and recording, and what they do when something goes wrong.
  • Note evidence, not ratings, while you circulate. "Read 2.3 not 2.30, corrected when asked" is usable later. A circled letter with no note is not.
  • Rate afterwards, once you have the whole picture. Trying to grade live means missing the next group.
  • A student who says "wait, that one looks different from the other two" is demonstrating the criterion as clearly as one who reads a scale correctly. Write it down.
No group starts without sign-off Every group states its own hazards and controls aloud before any equipment is issued, and you approve the plan on the spot. A plan you have not signed is a group that cannot start. This is assessable and it takes two minutes per group — do it while others are still settling.

Method notes

  • Tear pads into thirds. One pad makes three replicates. Two packs cover a class comfortably.
  • Vinegar soak is 2 minutes, then squeeze out hard. Soaking longer does not help; leaving it dripping wet slows the reaction because the air cannot reach the metal.
  • Treatments go on after the soak — that is the whole point of testing a protective coating.
  • Same size pieces. A group that does not control this has an obvious flaw to write about, which is fine, but most will want to fix it once asked.
  • Readings at 0, 10, 20 and 30 minutes. Use the same 0–4 coverage scale they have used for five weeks.
  • Photograph at every reading. Photos are the backup if a rating turns out to be inconsistent.
If a group's variable does not suit steel wool Most will be testing coatings, which works perfectly. A group testing something structural — the effect of a gap or scratch in a coating, say — may find steel wool awkward. Let them use a nail alongside the wool and take the wool as their main data, or pair them with the whole-class temperature demo. Do not redesign their plan on the spot.

The patchy-coating result is the most useful thing that can happen. It is exactly what the client needs to hear: a coating only protects while it is complete.

Overview

You wrote the plan. Today you run it — and this time nobody is telling you what to do next.

What we are doing today

Rust needs two things: water and air. Your nails proved that, but they took five weeks to do it.

Steel wool rusts much faster. It is made of thousands of thin threads, so there is far more metal touching the air than on a nail. What takes a nail five weeks takes steel wool about half an hour.

You have three pieces. Two get the treatment you chose. One stays bare, so you have something to compare against. Everything else about the three has to be the same, or you will not know whether it was your treatment that made the difference.

You watch them for half an hour and write down what changes. By the end you will know whether your treatment slowed the rust down. Next week you tell the client.

What we are learning

Learning intention

We are learning to carry out a repeatable investigation and record data precisely enough to be trusted.

I will be successful when I can…

  • follow my own plan, and adjust it when something does not work
  • measure and record with the precision the equipment allows
  • keep a record another person could check
  • find a weakness in a method I did not carry out myself, and in my own
  • work so that my results are repeatable

Stretch

  • I can run a second trial with one deliberate change, to test whether my first result holds.
  • I can spot an odd result while it is happening and write down why I think it happened.
  • I can name two things the other class could fairly criticise about our method.
Curriculum AC9S6I02 — plan and conduct repeatable investigations, including planning for the safe use of equipment and materials.
AC9S6I03 — use equipment to observe, measure and record data with reasonable precision.
AC9S6I04 — construct and use appropriate representations to organise and process data.
You are being watched today, and that is deliberate Part of your assessment is how you work, not just what you write afterwards. Whether you read the scale properly, whether you write things down as they happen, and what you do when something goes wrong — none of that survives into a report, so it gets noticed here instead.

Three jars, labels, marker
Why the vinegar first New steel wool has a thin coating on it that keeps the air off the metal. Vinegar takes that coating away. Once it is gone the air gets straight at the metal, so the rust starts in minutes instead of days. You saw this happen at the steel wool station in Lesson 1. This time you are doing it on purpose.

Before you sit back

  1. Are your three pieces the same size? Does it matter?
  2. Is anything different between your three jars apart from the treatment?
  3. What is your zero reading?
Why question 1 matters

A bigger piece has more surface, so it rusts faster — nothing to do with your treatment. If your pieces are noticeably different sizes, that is a real problem with your method. You can fix it now, or you can write about it honestly later. Both are acceptable. Pretending it did not happen is not.

Watch the treatment step This is where most methods drift. Groups apply a generous smear of jelly to one piece and a thin one to another, then compare them as though the only difference was the coating type. Ask "how are you making sure that is the same each time?" — do not fix it for them, but make sure they notice.

Run and record 40 min

Readings at 10, 20 and 30 minutes. Photograph every time. Between readings you work on the swap — the other class's graphs are on the next tab.

Rate each jar 0 to 4 against the scale you have been using for five weeks.

Rate all three separately. Do not average them in your head. If they disagree, that is data.

Every reading

  1. Look at all three jars.
  2. Rate each one, separately.
  3. Photograph from the same angle each time.
  4. Write anything odd in the notes column now, not later.
Write it down as it happens If something surprises you, write it down while it is still surprising. Nobody remembers accurately at the end of a lesson, and a note you wrote at the time is evidence. A memory is not.

Watch the front bench

One jar at the front has a thermometer in it and one piece of untreated steel wool. The temperature goes on the board as it changes.

  1. What is happening to the temperature?
  2. Nothing was heated. So where is the warmth coming from?
  3. Which of our evidence criteria does this match?
Show the answers

1. It rises, usually by 5 to 10 °C over about fifteen minutes.

2. From the rusting itself. When iron and oxygen join, energy is released — the reaction makes its own heat.

3. "It got hot" — from the list you built in Lesson 1. Back then it was something you noticed. Now it is a number on the board, which is a much stronger piece of evidence.

Why the temperature demo earns its bench space Some students still half-believe rusting is something that happens to metal rather than a reaction the metal takes part in. A thermometer climbing on its own, with no heat source anywhere near it, is hard to argue with. It also closes the loop on the Lesson 1 criteria chart — "it got hot" stops being a vague observation and becomes a measurement.

If something goes wrong

Write down what happened, when, and what you did about it. A method that failed and was honestly described is worth more marks than a tidy result you cannot explain.

The swap in the gaps, 20 min

Here are the other class's graphs from last week. You were not there. You do not know how they did it.

This runs while your jars sit Your steel wool needs ten minutes between readings and you are not needed in that time. Work on this between readings, and stop the moment a reading is due — the reading always wins.

They tested something you did not. You tested something they did not. Neither class can answer the client's question alone. But before you use their numbers, you have to decide whether you trust them.

Write these down before you discuss anything

  1. Two things you cannot tell from this data. What did they not write down that you would need to know?
  2. One thing you think may have gone wrong. Be specific — name the step.
  3. One question you would ask them if they were standing here.
Written first, talking afterwards Do this on your own paper before anyone says anything out loud. Once someone announces what they think, it gets very hard to notice anything different.
Things worth looking for
  • Did they dry the nails the same way you did? Would you know if they had not?
  • Did every jar start on the same day?
  • Were the jars all in the same place — same light, same temperature?
  • Did they use the same size nails?
  • Is one of their three jars wildly different from the other two? Did they say why?
  • Did they record to two decimal places, or round?
  • Is anything missing — a week's reading, a jar, a mass?

Now turn it around

What would the other class say about ours? Name two things they could fairly attack about our method.

The highest-value fifteen minutes in the unit Students critique another class in ways they will never critique their friends, and they cannot fall back on "I know what we meant". Insist on written critiques before any discussion — the writing is the evidence and the discussion is where they find out whether they were right.

The turn-it-around question is the harder half and the more important one. A student who can name a weakness in their own method is doing what the evaluation criterion actually asks. Expect resistance; most groups will defend their own data instinctively.

Why this matters

  1. Could either class have answered the client's question alone?
  2. Real scientists do this to each other constantly. Why would that make science better rather than worse?
Where this is going

No. One class found out what makes rust happen. The other found out what slows it down. The client needs both, and neither class had the time or the jars to do it all.

Scientists publish their methods precisely so other people can attack them. A finding that survives other people trying to pull it apart is worth far more than one nobody checked. That is not scientists being difficult with each other — it is the part that makes the answer trustworthy.

AC9S6H01, evidenced This is "examine why advances in science are often the result of collaboration or build on the work of others" — done, not narrated. Students have just experienced needing someone else's data and having to judge whether to trust it. Name it explicitly so they can write about it in CP3, because they will need it there.

Plan the report 15 min

Next week you write to [CLIENT NAME]. Work out now what you are missing.

What you will need

  • Today's data, all four readings
  • Next week's final reading — taken at the start of the lesson
  • Your graph from Week 5
  • The other class's data
  • Your photographs

Work it out as a group

  1. Based on what you have seen so far, what are you going to recommend?
  2. What is the strongest piece of evidence you have for that?
  3. What is the weakest part of your method — the bit they could fairly attack?
  4. What do you still need before you can write it?
Question 3 is not a trap Every method has a weak point. Naming it yourself is worth marks; having it pointed out to you is not. You spent twenty minutes last week finding holes in another class's work — do the same to your own now, while you still have time to say something sensible about it.

Agree who is doing what

You each write your own report, but the group shares the data and does the defence together. Decide now who is bringing what next week, and who is answering which challenge question.

Use this phase to triage Circulate and listen for groups whose data will not support any recommendation — usually because every jar rusted the same, or nothing rusted at all. They need to know now, not next Tuesday. A group in that position should be pointed toward the class dataset and the other class's results, and should plan to write about why their own test was inconclusive. That is a legitimate report and it can score well on evaluation.

Secure 10 min

Storing for the week

  1. Check every jar is labelled: group, treatment, date.
  2. Lids on the jars that had lids on today. Leave open the ones that were open.
  3. Onto the shelf, together, out of the walkway.
  4. Photograph the shelf so you know what it looked like when you left it.
Do not change anything between now and next week If you move a jar, open it, or top it up, your final reading is measuring something different from your first. Leave them exactly as they are.

Clearing up

  • Vinegar down the sink with the tap running.
  • Steel wool into the bin, not the sink — it blocks drains and it rusts.
  • Benches wiped, trays stacked, glasses back.
  • Wash hands.

Before you go

Hand in your data sheet. You get it back next week. If it is in your bag, it will not be there when you need it.

Collect the data sheets Do not let students take them home. A lost sheet in Week 7 means a student with no data and nothing to write, and there is no time to recover it. Photograph or photocopy the set if you want a backup.

Next week

Final reading first thing, then the report. You will present to the group and be asked hard questions about how you know what you claim to know.
