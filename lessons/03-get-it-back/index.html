Get it back

Recovering a dissolved substance, and accounting for what is missing

AC9S6U04 AC9S6I02 AC9S6I03 AC9S6I05 90 minutes Term 4 · Week 3

Teaching notes

Read before the lesson. Hidden from students when teacher mode is off.

Where this sits Last week students said the salt was still in the water. Today they have to prove it, and the proof is a number on a scale. This lesson also does the quiet work of the whole unit: it is the first time students meet the idea that a measurement can be wrong without anybody having done anything stupid.

The one thing to protect

Do not give them the method. The challenge is stated, the equipment is on the bench, and they work it out. Filtering then evaporating is not a difficult sequence to reason toward if you have seen sand and seen a kettle — but it has to be theirs, because in Week 5 they plan an investigation with nobody holding their hand and this is the rehearsal.

Timing is the real risk in this lesson Evaporating 50 mL takes 22 to 36 minutes depending on how much heat actually reaches the basin. The lesson is built so heating starts at about the 25-minute mark and the resin discussion fills the wait. If heating has not started by the 40-minute mark, you will not get a dry basin. Push groups through the planning phase and get liquid onto heat.

Why 50 mL and 8 g

  • 50 mL is the most that will evaporate inside the lesson. The original plan said 100 mL — that would take the best part of an hour and the lesson would die waiting.
  • 8 g is enough that a realistic loss of half a gram to a gram is actually readable on 1 g scales. Any less and the losses vanish into rounding, and the yield discussion has nothing to work with.
  • 8 g in 50 mL is comfortably below saturation, so nothing crystallises out on the filter paper while they work.

The two moments that matter

1 · When the first basin comes off dry

There is white solid in the bottom of a dish that had clear liquid in it. Hold it up. Ask what it is and how they could check. Taste is not available — but they can look at the crystals, and they can weigh them. This is the answer to the question they were left with last week.

2 · When nobody gets 8 g back

Every group comes in under. The instinct is to feel they did it wrong. The move is to ask whether the salt was destroyed — no, it was measured badly or left behind. That distinction, between a chemistry problem and a measurement problem, is the whole of the error criterion in the assessment.

Method notes that decide whether this works

  • Weigh the empty basin first. Recovered salt = final mass minus empty mass. Groups that forget this have no result.
  • Use a sand bath. The basins are round-bottomed, so on the flat frypan they touch at one point, heat poorly and rock. Put 1.5–2 cm of dry sand in the pan and twist each basin down into it. That gives contact across the whole curve, holds the basin steady, and buffers the heat so the porcelain is not shocked and the salt does not spit. Not a water bath — that caps at 100 °C and roughly doubles the time.
  • Take the basin off while a damp crust remains. Residual heat finishes it. Left too long, the salt spits and you lose some over the side.
  • Let the basin cool before weighing. A hot dish sets up convection over the pan and gives a false low reading. Five minutes on a heat mat. This is worth naming out loud as a source of error rather than just enforcing.
  • You are the only person who touches the frypan. Basins placed and removed by you with tongs.
If the basins are not dry when you need them Weigh whichever are ready, run the discussion on those, and put the rest on a sunny windowsill. Weigh them at the start of Lesson 4 and add the numbers to the board then. Say plainly why it happened. A teacher modelling "we ran out of time and the measurement was not ready" is worth more than a fudged number.

What to expect

RecoveredYieldWhat it means
7 gabout 88%Typical good result. Losses on the filter paper and in transfer.
6 gabout 75%Common. Solution left in the beaker or soaked into the filter paper.
5 g or lessunder 63%Something substantial was left behind. Ask where.
9 g or moreover 100%Not dry. Water is being weighed as salt. Back on the heat.

The over-100% result is the most useful one if it happens. It cannot be right, everyone can see it cannot be right, and working out why is better thinking than any correct answer on the sheet.

On the resin content

Handle with care and be accurate Plant resins used as adhesives are named explicitly in the v9 elaborations as an example of First Nations Australians' knowledges of reversible processes. Present it as technology — a material chosen deliberately because heating softens it and cooling sets it, which means a tool can be repaired in the field years later with nothing but a fire. Name the practice generally rather than attributing it to a particular group unless you have a sourced local reference. Do not invent a local attribution, and do not let it become a five-minute aside — it is a genuine example of the science in this lesson.

MisconceptionHow it shows todayResponse

What we are learning

Learning intention

We are learning to recover a dissolved substance, and to account for how much of it we get back.

I will be successful when I can…

  • separate an insoluble solid from a mixture by filtering
  • recover a dissolved solid by evaporating the water
  • compare what I started with to what I recovered
  • give a reason for the difference between the two

Stretch

  • I can work out my recovery as a percentage of what I started with.
  • I can propose one specific change to the method that would improve it, and say why.
Curriculum AC9S6U04 — compare reversible changes, including dissolving and changes of state… Elaboration: describing how dissolved substances are reclaimed from solutions.
AC9S6I02 — plan and conduct repeatable investigations, including describing potential risks and planning for the safe use of equipment.
AC9S6I05 — recognise possible sources of error and select evidence to support reasoned conclusions.

The challenge 8 min

In this beaker there is 8 grams of salt, 50 millilitres of water, and a spoonful of sand. They are all mixed together.

Get the salt back. All of it, dry, in the dish. You will be weighed.

Two ways to get the sand out

Half the class will use one, half the other. We are going to find out which one loses less salt.

Filtering1

Pour the whole mixture through a paper cone in a funnel. The sand stays on the paper, the liquid runs through.

Funnel, filter paper, second beaker
Decanting2

Let the sand settle to the bottom, then carefully pour the liquid off it into a second beaker. No paper at all.

Two beakers, patience

Before you plan

  1. Which of the three things in that beaker is dissolved, and which is not?
  2. Why does that difference matter for getting the salt out?
  3. A funnel on its own, with no paper in it — would that separate anything? Why not?
  4. Which method do you think will lose more salt, and where would it be lost?
  5. What is the one thing you have to do first, and why does the order matter?
Show the answers

1. The salt is dissolved. The sand is not — you can still see it sitting there.

2. Anything still in visible pieces can be caught by a filter or left behind when you pour. Anything dissolved travels with the water wherever the water goes.

3. No. A funnel is just a cone that directs liquid into a narrow opening. It has nothing in it to hold anything back, so the sand would pour straight through with the water. The paper is what does the separating.

4. Open question — that is what we are finding out. Filtering loses salt in the solution soaked into the paper. Decanting loses salt in the liquid you have to leave behind so the sand does not come with it.

5. Get the sand out first, then evaporate. The other way round and you end up with salt and sand mixed in the dish, and no way to separate them.

Question 3 is worth pressing on Students often assume a funnel filters. It does not — it is a shape, not a barrier. Getting this said out loud means the filter paper is understood as the working part rather than an accessory, which matters when they explain their losses later.
Do not reveal which method wins Most students predict the paper loses more, because they can see it getting wet. The data usually says the opposite — decanting forces you to abandon five to ten millilitres of solution, while the paper holds back one or two. Let the numbers do it.
Before any equipment moves Safety glasses on. Nothing is tasted, at any point, for any reason. The frypan is teacher-only and you stay behind the line.

Plan and critique 12 min

Your method is assigned, not chosen Half the groups filter and half decant. That is what makes the comparison fair — if everyone picked their favourite we would learn nothing about which one actually loses less.

Draft it — 6 minutes

On your whiteboard, number the steps for your method. Someone from another group has to be able to follow it.

Do not forget Somewhere in your method there needs to be a step that says weigh the empty basin. If you do not know what the dish weighs by itself, you cannot work out how much salt is in it at the end.

Swap — 6 minutes

Pass your whiteboard to another group. Read their method as if you had to carry it out.

Write one question about a step that would not work, or that you could not follow. Not a compliment. A question.

  1. Is there a step missing?
  2. Is anything in the wrong order?
  3. Is there a point where salt could get left behind without them noticing?

Get your board back. Fix what they found. Then you get your equipment.

This is the rehearsal for Week 5 The same protocol — draft, swap, critique in writing, revise — is how CP2 is assessed. Doing it here on a low-stakes task means that in Week 5 the procedure is familiar and students can spend their attention on the science instead of on what you are asking them to do.
Keep this to twelve minutes It will want to run long and it cannot. Heating has to start by the 40-minute mark. If a group's method is still wrong at the end of the swap, let them run it — a method that fails produces better evidence for the error discussion than one you corrected for them.

Separate and heat 15 min

Move quickly. The water needs as long as possible on the heat.

Step 1 — weigh the empty basin1

Before anything goes in it. Write the number down. Without this you have no result at all.

Evaporating basin, scales
Step 2 — your method2

Filtering: fold the paper into a cone, sit it in the funnel, funnel over the empty beaker. Pour slowly and keep the liquid below the top of the paper — overfill it and liquid runs down the outside and misses the paper entirely.

Decanting: let the sand settle completely first. Then tip slowly and steadily, and stop the moment sand starts moving toward the lip. Do not chase the last drop.

Filter: funnel + paper. Decant: two beakers and a steady hand.
Step 3 — into the basin3

Pour your separated liquid into the weighed basin. It should be clear.

Get every drop across. Anything left in the beaker is salt you will never see again.

Basin, separated liquid
Step 4 — onto the heat4

Bring your basin to the line and hand it over. The teacher sets it into the sand and takes it out.

Then sit down. It takes about twenty-five minutes.

Teacher operates the frypan throughout. Students stay behind the line.
Look at what you have got Your liquid is clear. The sand is gone — either on the paper or left in the bottom of the first beaker. But the salt came with the water. It is still in there, invisible, exactly as it was last week. Separating the sand did not touch it.

Before you sit down

  1. Where is the sand now?
  2. The liquid that came through is clear. Is it just water?
  3. Look at what you left behind. Is any of it wet? What is in that liquid?
  4. Why did the sand get separated out but the salt did not?
Show the answers

1. Filtering — on the paper. Decanting — in the bottom of the first beaker.

2. No. It is a salt solution. Clear does not mean pure, and you proved that last week when the mass did not change.

3. Yes — the paper is soaked, or there is liquid sitting on the sand. That liquid is salt solution, and every millilitre of it is salt you are not going to recover.

4. The sand is in pieces big enough to be caught by paper or to sink to the bottom. The dissolved salt is spread through the water in pieces far too small for either. Both methods sort by size — and dissolved things are below the cut.

The two failures to watch for Filtering groups overfilling the cone, so liquid runs between the paper and the funnel and takes sand with it. Decanting groups being impatient and pouring before the sand has settled. Walk the room — both are visible from across the bench.

While it evaporates 12 min

A reversible change that people chose on purpose, and used for thousands of years.

Across Australia, First Nations peoples made adhesives from plant resin — including resin from spinifex and from grass trees.

The resin was worked with heat. Warm it and it softens. Let it cool and it sets hard. It holds a stone point onto a spear shaft, a blade into a handle, a barb onto a hook.

The part that matters

Years later, out on Country, a long way from anywhere, the tool breaks.

You do not need a new one. You warm the resin again, it softens again, you reset the point, and it hardens again. The same material, as many times as you need.

Answer these on your sheet Section 7 of your Lesson 3 sheet in OneNote — Plant resin, the part that matters. Write straight onto the sheet.
  1. Is softening and resetting resin a reversible or an irreversible change? How do you know?
  2. Which change we have studied is it most like — melting chocolate, or toasting bread? Say why.
  3. Why would being reversible be exactly the property you would want in a glue?
  4. Superglue cannot be undone. When would that be better, and when would it be worse?
Show the answers

1. Reversible. Nothing new is made — the same resin goes soft and hard, over and over.

2. Melting chocolate. A change of state driven by heat, with the substance unchanged.

3. Because it can be repaired anywhere, with a fire, using the glue that is already on the tool. An irreversible glue means a broken tool is finished, and you need new materials to replace it.

4. Irreversible is better when you never want it to come apart — a boat, a building. It is worse when the thing will need maintaining, or when you are a long way from replacements.

The point This is not a simpler version of glue. The reversibility is the engineering. A material was selected because of a property we have spent three weeks learning to identify — and it was selected a very long time before anybody wrote it down in a science book.
Keep it accurate Name the practice generally — First Nations peoples across Australia used plant resins as adhesives — rather than attributing it to a particular group unless you have a sourced local reference to hand. Present it as technology and materials science, which is what it is. Resist turning it into a story.
Watch the pans while this runs You are talking and the basins are heating. Check them every few minutes. Pull any basin that is down to a damp crust — the residual heat finishes the job and leaving it on causes the salt to spit over the side.

Show the answers

1–2. Most groups recover 6 to 7 grams. Everybody is under 8.

3. Salt. It looks like salt, it appeared where a salt solution used to be, nothing was added, and it weighs roughly what went in. No single one of those settles it — together they are strong.

4. No. Mass does not vanish — you proved that last week. It is somewhere in this room: on the filter paper, in a beaker, spat over the side of a basin.

Which method won? 20 min

Both columns go on the board

Filtering groups on one side, decanting groups on the other. Nobody got 8 grams.

MethodTypical recoveryWhere the salt went
Filtering7.4 – 7.8 gSoaked into the paper and the wet sand on it.
Decanting6.1 – 7.4 gLeft behind in the liquid sitting on the sand.
  1. Which method got more salt back?
  2. Was that what you predicted? Why did you think the other one?
  3. Name the exact place each method loses its salt.
  4. Two groups used the same method and got different numbers. Does that mean one did it wrong?
  5. What one change would improve either method?
Show the answers

1. Filtering, usually by half a gram to a gram.

2. Most people predict the paper loses more, because you can see it getting wet. But a filter paper only holds a millilitre or two. Decanting makes you abandon five to ten millilitres, or the sand comes across with the liquid.

3. Filtering — in the paper and the damp sand on it. Decanting — in the liquid you deliberately left in the first beaker.

4. No. Different hands, different pouring, different patience. A different number is a reason to ask what they did differently, not to assume somebody failed.

5. Rinse. Pour a little clean water through the paper, or over the settled sand, and add that to the basin too. It sounds like cheating. It is not — the salt in that rinse was always yours, and you are only adding water, which is going to evaporate anyway.

The big idea Neither method is wrong. They lose salt in different places, and until you compared them you had no way of knowing which place costs more. A number being off does not mean somebody was careless — it means the method lost some, and the job is to say where.
This is the skill CP3 marks Students have just compared methods with those of others and identified where each one loses material. That is the Interpreting and Evaluating criterion, in miniature, on a task where they can actually see the mechanism. Use the phrase source of error out loud. In Week 5 they do the same thing to another class's rust data, where the mechanism is invisible and they have to reason about it.
If the class result comes out the other way It can. A careful decanting group can beat a sloppy filtering group, and if your filter papers are large and thick they hold more. Do not bend the discussion toward the expected answer — ask what would explain the result you actually got. That is a better lesson than the tidy one.

Exit ticket

Two groups both started with 8 g of salt. The group that filtered got 7.5 g back. The group that decanted got 6.5 g. Neither group did anything wrong. Explain how that is possible.

Sorting the exit tickets into piles

Ready to move on — names where each method loses salt and says mass is not destroyed. Bonus for noting the decanting group had to leave more liquid behind.

Watch in Lesson 4 — knows nothing was destroyed but cannot name a place, or says one method is "just better" without a mechanism.

Target directly — says the decanting group must have made a mistake, or that a gram of salt was destroyed. Both mean the Lesson 2 idea has not carried, and CP1 is two weeks away.
